Hybrid Optical Pumping

Testing how density imbalance affects spin-temperature equilibrium and polarization transfer in hybrid alkali-vapour cells.

Project Abstract

At A*STAR Q.InC, I designed an experiment to investigate a common assumption in hybrid alkali-vapour cells: that spin-exchange collisions are sufficiently rapid to maintain spin-temperature equilibrium between the atomic species. By identifying when this assumption breaks down, we can better understand the limits and optimal operating conditions of hybrid pumping, with the aim of producing more homogeneous polarization and sensor response.

The experimental scheme is related to the atomic-magnetometer projects described elsewhere in this notebook, but focuses on vapour-cell characteristics and responses rather than magnetic sensitivity.

Contribution & Recognition

I developed the experimental plan for comparing hybrid cells with different potassium-to-rubidium density ratios. The work brought together cell fabrication, absorption-based density measurements, and two polarization measurement approaches, using potassium and rubidium readout.

This project was carried out with the Atomic Vapor Cell Group at A*STAR's Quantum Innovation Centre (Q.InC). The supplied project materials do not identify a separate award or formal recognition.

Background And Motivation

Hybrid pumping is an adaptation of optical pumping aimed primarily at improving the uniformity of atomic polarisation. Optical pumping transfers angular momentum from circularly polarised photons to an atomic medium through resonant absorption, producing a net polarisation along the quantisation axis. In a particularly dense medium, however, absorption can create a polarisation gradient: it is strongest on the side where the light enters and weakest at the exit.

Hybrid pumping addresses this gradient by optically pumping a “donor” species that is relatively optically thin. Spin-exchange collisions then transfer polarisation to an optically thick “receiver” species. This can produce more uniform receiver polarisation while preserving its signal coherence, even when the pump light does not penetrate the receiver cell uniformly.

This approach often assumes that spin-exchange collisions are sufficiently rapid to maintain spin-temperature equilibrium between the species. However, spin-exchange rates depend on the density of collision partners and their collisional cross-section. Reducing potassium density to improve pumping uniformity may therefore cause the potassium and rubidium polarisations to diverge. The experiment was designed to measure both species’ densities and polarisations across cells with different density ratios, testing where the equilibrium assumption breaks down.

The longer-term aim is to establish when hybrid pumping remains effective and identify operating conditions that improve vapour-cell uniformity and atomic magnetometer sensitivity.

Results And Next Steps

Initial cell-characterization work includes potassium absorption scans and an absorption-fit estimate of the rubidium number density in the Elsa cell: approximately 1.141 × 1014 cm−3 in the supplied 23 July 2025 analysis. The characterization figures also document potassium transmission/absorption and measured magnetic-field gradients across the probe, pump, and cell axes.

A supplied polarization estimate compares 100 °C and 150 °C operation as pump power increases. It suggests that polarization rises with pump power in both cases, reaching roughly 0.9 at the upper end of the plotted range. This is an estimate, not a direct measurement of the density-ratio dependence that motivates the project.

The central next step is to complete matched density and polarization measurements for the planned cell set, then compare potassium and rubidium polarization against the measured density ratio. This will establish whether and where the spin-temperature approximation breaks down, and how that affects hybrid-pumping uniformity.

Methodology

Cell and density characterization

The project plan called for hybrid vapour cells with different potassium-to-rubidium density ratios. Absorption spectroscopy provides an estimate of each species' number density. For the potassium characterization shown here, the cell is heated, a potassium laser is sent through the cell, and its frequency is swept across an absorption resonance. Comparing the transmitted signal with a reference scan reveals the absorption line, which can be fitted to estimate the vapour density.

The supplied Elsa analysis includes a rubidium absorption fit and a potassium D1 absorption scan. These characterize the cell and measurement response; they do not by themselves establish the final relationship between density ratio and hybrid-pumping efficiency.

Polarization and field measurements

The planned comparison measures polarization through two routes: one using potassium and one using rubidium. Faraday rotation provides an optical readout of the atoms' spin polarization. Recording both species allows their polarization to be compared directly as the density ratio changes.

Magnetic-field gradients across the cell are also characterized because spatial field variation can affect the measured resonance and polarization. The supplied gradient plot reports the fitted field variation along the probe, pump, and third cell axis, providing a map of an important experimental condition.
